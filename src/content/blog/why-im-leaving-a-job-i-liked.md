Here's the version of this post I'm not writing: a vague LinkedIn announcement about "new opportunities" and "exciting chapters," timed for maximum ambiguity about whether I quit, got pushed, or something in between.

Here's what actually happened. I spent the last year as Senior Azure and AI Development Engineer at The Vizius Group, a cybersecurity firm in Greenville. I built an Azure Service Bus payroll automation with a 45-day handoff. I migrated a production app between Azure tenants on zero-credential OIDC pipelines. I open-sourced a Python library — `azure-bootstrap`, since renamed `vibey-bootstrap` — that's now used across the org's Functions repos. I liked the work. I liked the team. And at the end of August 2026, I'm leaving, because we both looked honestly at the pipeline of AI work coming through the door and agreed there wasn't enough of it to justify keeping a Senior AI Development Engineer on staff long-term.

That's it. That's the whole story. No drama, no burned bridge, no vague euphemism doing the work an honest sentence should do.

## Why I'm telling you the boring, true version

I'm autistic. I was diagnosed in 2025, and it didn't change how I think — it explained it. One of the things it explained is a fairly strong allergy to exactly the kind of social fiction that career announcements usually run on. I can write "excited for what's next" as well as anyone. I'd rather tell you what actually happened, because the honest version is more useful to you and more sustainable for me.

There's a finding I keep coming back to, from a 2024 study in *Frontiers in Psychology*: autistic employees show measurably lower moral disengagement and are less susceptible to the bystander effect than their neurotypical peers — an "ethical advantage," the researchers called it. I think the same wiring shows up here, in miniature. I don't have a strong instinct to smooth over an ordinary business reality with a nicer-sounding story, even when the nicer-sounding story is the norm. The gap between "this happened" and "this is how I'm describing it" registers as an error condition I'd rather just fix than manage.

## What I'm actually good at, named plainly

Thirteen-plus years of software engineering, the last several of it increasingly narrow and increasingly deep: RAG systems, event-driven Azure microservices, automation pipelines built to be handed off rather than hoarded. I've built self-hosted Mistral-7B RAG infrastructure with zero external dependencies for a client that couldn't send data anywhere. I've walked into a 59,000-line AI-built codebase, assessed it in ten hours, and delivered a phased Onion Architecture roadmap a real team could actually execute. The hyperfocus that makes small talk exhausting is the same mechanism that lets me sit inside one hard architecture problem until it's actually solved, not just patched.

That's not a compensation story. It's the mechanism.

## What comes next

Starting September 2026, I'm looking for the next team — Greenville, SC-remote first, open to remote anywhere in the US — where AI, automation, and architecture are the whole job, not a promising side project competing for headcount against everything else a cybersecurity firm needs to do. If that's a role you have, or a role you're about to open, I'd genuinely like to talk. Not "let's grab coffee sometime" talk. Actual "here's my résumé and here's what I've shipped" talk.

I don't have a tidy inspirational close for this one. I liked the job. I'm leaving anyway, because the math didn't work, and pretending otherwise wouldn't have helped either of us. That's not a tragedy. It's just what's true, said plainly — which, autism aside, is probably still the best way to end a post like this.
